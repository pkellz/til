## Gaining courage to offer solutions without fear of rejection

You're in a team meeting discussing solutions to problem "X". Your teammates are discussing something and a light bulb goes off in your head - a solution that everyone else seems to be missing. You consider chiming in, but you hesitate... 
- "I haven't had much time to think this all the way through. What if I missed something?"
- "My solution seems ***too*** easy. Why hasn't anyone else thought of it? Is it possible my idea is just **OBVIOUSLY** wrong / bad practice? And that it's already common knowledge for everyone else?"
- "I'll just wait and see what others say so I can get more information." 

(*Spoiler*: Even with taking in more and more of other's thoughts, you never conjure up the confidence to share your ***own*** ideas. You just end up in a perpetual thought loop of weighing other people's solutions against your own [unspoken] one. At some point you may even convince yourself "Ah, yeah that person's idea was way better than what I was thinking anyway. See, I didn't even have to say anything". You may even be secretly *hoping* this is the case.
Or on the other hand, the more you listen the more holes you spot in other people's logic and alarm bells in your head go "that won't work because of ____!". But you remain silent. ["I still could be wrong about... let me listen some more."])

Unfortunately, the meeting ends without you saying anything. The reason people tend to clam up in these situations is they're afraid to look foolish if someone pokes holes in their solution or points out flaws in their assumptions. People often feel directly connected with the result of their labor. The more time, energy, brainpower, tears, sweat, blood etc. you spend on something the more emotionally interconnected you feel to that thing.  

> "This is especially true when it comes to one's ideas. Such that a rejection of your idea feels, in a way, like a rejection of you."

When people poke holes in and challenge your ideas, it can feel like a direct personal affront. By offering your ideas to the world, you're basically putting your own self at risk - at risk of rejection, of embarrassment, of scrutiny. And that's what makes it hard.

This is a natural reaction. One way to mitigate this is by removing your **self** from the situation. Disconnect yourself from your ideas. Consider your ideas as a utility - merely tools that can help solve your immediate problem. They are disposable and are not a reflection of your worth as a person. They are either useful or dead weight. One rhetorical tool to help with this is to **_lead with your gut_**:

> "My gut is telling me…" 

> "My gut reaction is that…" 

> "My uninformed gut is telling me…"

By introducing the idea with "your gut", you disconnect yourself from the situation. ***Your gut is not you***. Think of your gut as a close, generally reliable (but not perfect) advisor to you. Your listeners already recognize that guts are sometimes unreliable (because they've dealth _with their own_ unreliable guts!)

Your listeners will instinctively understand and 100% respect the fact that gut-born ideas are not meant to be perfect - merely a foundation on which to continue building the conversation. If your gut produces an idea that, after some scrutiny, was not the right way to go... "Oh well. That was just me spitballin'. Let's go with your idea, Jim". Remember, ideas are just utilities. Keep them if they are useful. Otherwise dispose of them.

The benefits of leading with your gut are:
1) You gain the confidence to introduce your (good, but probably imperfect) ideas.
2) Over time you will naturally appear more senior. Your team will come to realize you have a natural & immediate grasp on problems in your domain and will learn to trust your gut instinctively.

> 🚨 ***WARNING***! #2 only applies when your gut is generally ***correct***! This principle can work against you, too. If you're gut is consistently wrong / misguided about the correct way to solve a problem, people will learn to not trust you. You don't want to be known as the guy who is very eager to offer solutions, but does not often have the right ideas.
